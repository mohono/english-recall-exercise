Since 2019, over a thousand imposing warehouse campuses have popped up around the world. Each complex covers nearly 100,000 square meters, uses as much water as a small town, and as much electricity as a small city. These are AI data centers— facilities for running and training Large Language Models like Claude and ChatGPT. We’ve had similar data centers dedicated to operating the internet since the 2000s, but these customized complexes come with a whole new suite of challenges and possibilities. AI data centers are generally organized into several zones. The Network Operations Center manages data traffic and power consumption. Power systems including transformers, gas generators, and battery-powered backups ensure uninterrupted electricity flow. And a connectivity hub called the Meet-Me-Room directs incoming data into the heart of the data center: the server room. When you ask an LLM a question, your prompt enters one of these server rooms. It's too complicated to explore how that model responds right now, but it involves doing tons of math. To manage these calculations, a data center relies on thousands of server racks, each stacked with trays containing server boards that house memory chips and GPUs. These powerhouse microchips use thousands of tiny computing cores working in parallel to do trillions of mathematical operations per second. While most prompts only require a few GPUs to answer, data centers already field tens of thousands of prompts a second. And that rate is growing fast. Plus, training LLMS requires hundreds of thousands of GPUs working together, making server grids like these essential to get an AI up and running. However, all this energy-intensive work leads to a major problem: heat. Even in climate-controlled server rooms, GPUs get hot enough to melt server boards. And the copper wires connecting these chips constantly dissipate energy, using more electricity and generating additional heat. Fortunately, engineers are on the job. To cool chips, they’re using tried-and-true solutions like water towers that absorb and evaporate heat alongside new approaches like piping chilled water behind server boards, or dunking entire trays into non-conductive cooling liquids. At the same time, they're replacing inefficient copper wires that transmit data through electricity with optical fibers that transmit data through light. At the top of many racks, optical transceivers convert electrical data to light signals, which travel through optical fibers with higher data fidelity than copper and near-zero power loss. And as photonics technology gets further integrated inside servers, it can solve another major problem. GPUs only have so much space for copper connections, limiting how much data they transmit at once. But integrated photonic chips can use different wavelengths of light to send multiple streams of data over a few optical lines. This same tech could even let GPUs interact with memory chips on neighboring trays and racks— spreading out their heat load and allowing bigger LLMs to use less power. While some experts believe integrated photonics will reduce data center power use by nearly 50%, this still might not be enough. GPUs run at roughly 16 light bulbs worth of energy. Multiply that by every GPU in a data center, and you're looking at enough energy to power 300,000 homes. Companies are also pursuing ever-smarter AI, which, so far, has meant ever-larger AI. Even with increasing efficiencies, larger models require bigger data centers and more electricity. Some estimates predict that by 2028, US data centers alone could be using as much energy as eight New York Citys. In fact, some AI companies have already proposed pairing next-gen data centers with their own personal nuclear power plant. Clearly, we don’t have enough energy to support this rapid expansion, so we either need to hit the brakes or find ways to compute smarter. For example, while current LLMs are designed to answer every possible question, you don’t need a model that knows Shakespeare to help you fix your dishwasher. We could design topic-specific “small language models” stored in micro-data centers. Alternately, tomorrow’s LLMs might employ new forms of optical computing that cut energy costs by using light itself to do the math. But it won’t matter how efficient these systems get if our hunger for AI keeps increasing. Ultimately, this isn’t a technical problem, it’s a social one: just how many resources are we willing to spend to build smarter machines?
