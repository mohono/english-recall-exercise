Morgan and her twin sister Lisa have been inseparable since birth. So nobody was surprised when they moved into identical houses right next to each other. The only difference? Morgan bought her house, while Lisa is renting hers. For generations, conventional wisdom has been that home ownership is financially better than renting. But let’s follow the twins and see how things play out over the years, based on historical trends in the US. As Morgan and Lisa move into their two-bedroom houses, their upfront costs look a little different. Morgan buys her house for $400,000. Like most buyers, she takes out a mortgage— a loan that she'll pay back to a lender over time with interest. For this, she needs to make a down payment— a lump sum that’s some percentage of the total home price. Between Morgan’s 10% down payment and a few extra fees, known as closing costs, Morgan spends a total of $50,000. Next door, Lisa signs a 12-month lease to rent her house for $1,800 a month. To get her keys, she’ll also pay a security deposit, bringing her total to $3,600— just a fraction of Morgan’s payment. Five years in, Morgan and Lisa have added a few more members to the family. Lisa’s rent has been steadily climbing, about 2% every year. Her monthly rent is now over $2,000, including a new pet fee. This brings her five year total to around $112,000. Meanwhile, Morgan’s still paying off her mortgage, a 30-year loan with a fixed interest rate of 6%. This type of long-term fixed-rate loan is common in the US, though loans with adjustable rates over shorter periods are much more typical in the rest of the world. Longer mortgages lower monthly payments by spreading them over an extended period. Morgan's monthly total, including taxes and insurance, is around $2,500 per month. But a large fraction of early payments goes towards interest. So five years in, she’s spent $150,000 on her property— $38,000 more than Lisa. Despite this, she's only chipped about $25,000 off her mortgage. The good news for Morgan is that the market has changed and interest rates have dropped, allowing her to refinance. Basically, she takes out a new mortgage at the lower interest rate to replace her old one. Her new rate is 5%, bringing her total monthly cost down to around $2,300. 15 years in, Lisa’s rent now exceeds Morgan’s monthly payments. But this isn’t the only cost to consider. During an intense storm season, flash floods hit their neighborhood. As a homeowner, Morgan pays for repairs, while Lisa lets her landlord handle everything. At this point, Morgan has spent over $500,000 on her house, while Lisa has paid around $380,000. After 30 years, Morgan is finally done paying off her mortgage. To get there, she spent about $1 million, including the price of the house, interest, taxes, and insurance. But she's also had to pay for repairs and upkeep, bringing her total to $1.4 million. Meanwhile, Lisa’s rent is now $3,300 a month, and she's spent around $900,000 in rent. But before we calculate final tallies, there’s one more cost to consider: opportunity cost. When Morgan chose to spend $40,000 on a down payment, she missed out on putting that $40,000 into a stable, diversified retirement account. That’s what Lisa did, and it’s grown into $500,000. Morgan’s not sweating it, because her home turned out to be a pretty good investment, as it's now worth $1.3 million. In the end, Morgan spent $1.4 million on a $1.3 million house, putting her at a loss of $100,000. Lisa spent $900,000 in rent, but made $460,000 in investments, putting her at a total loss of $440,000. In this situation, Morgan came out on top. Plus, she’s paid off her mortgage, so she only needs to pay taxes, insurance, and maintenance costs going forward. But Lisa won out in other ways. She had lower monthly expenses for over 10 years, which allowed her to spend on hobbies and travel. In another scenario, she might have put that extra cash into retirement or investments and allowed it to grow. And Lisa wasn't tied to one location. In fact, she just got a job opportunity in a new city and is finally feeling ready to branch out.
