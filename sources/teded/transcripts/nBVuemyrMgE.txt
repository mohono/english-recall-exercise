In 2018, Olympic National Park had a goat problem. Mountain goats, which were invasive to the area, were charging at visitors and trampling rare plants. They were after one thing: salt. As hikers worked up a sweat or relieved themselves along trails, they created an irresistible draw for these salt-hungry animals. The situation became so dangerous that crews had to capture hundreds of goats and have them airlifted out of the park. These goats’ behavior may seem exceptional, but all animals rely on salt, or sodium chloride, for many basic biological functions. An influx of sodium, for example, is the trigger that causes nerve cells to fire and relay messages. While chloride is a key component in stomach acid that helps digest food. And humans, just like those salt-crazed goats, have gone to similarly great lengths to feed their own salt fixes. Our hunter-gatherer ancestors satisfied the need for salt primarily by eating other animals. Plants also contain salt, but far less. So, when agriculture flourished around 11,000 years ago, the practice of adding salt to food began. Seaside civilizations filled shallow basins with seawater to evaporate in the sun, leaving salt crystals behind. Inland, ancient settlers along a salty river in central China boiled river water in vats to evaporate the water and harvest the salt crystals. Meanwhile, miners in what's today Hallstatt, Austria, cut into mountains to harvest rock salt, long buried in the earth after ancient seas evaporated. Salt wasn't just a biological necessity, but an essential tool, allowing ancient civilizations to preserve fish, meats, produce, and dairy. Salt kills the bacteria that would otherwise spoil food by sucking water from these microbe’s cells. And salt was also prized for making food taste better. A pinch will mask bitter notes and bring out sour, sweet, and umami flavors. A heavier pour will mask sweetness and up the umami. However, exactly how salt performs these transformations is still a mystery. So wherever salt was plentiful, humans harvested it to trade with salt-hungry regions. As early as the 1st millennia CE, huge caravans of camels moved slabs of salt mined in the Sahara to West Africa. Here, salt supplies were so rare that people handed over equal quantities of gold dust for the precious commodity. In ancient Rome, the mineral was so valued that it’s believed that soldiers received part of their pay in salt— which may be why the word salary contains “sal,” the Latin root for salt. As for salt on tables, the first known containers for dispensing salt, called salt cellars, appeared in ancient Greece. Many scholars believe that at banquets in medieval Europe, these elaborate cellars marked social status, as the most important society members sat on one end of the table above the salt and the less important sat below it. But over the years, unequal access to salt didn’t go uncontested. In the 18th century, an unpopular salt tax in France, which nobility was exempt from paying, helped spur the French Revolution. In 1930, Gandhi’s salt march in protest of British laws that forbade Indians from collecting or selling salt on their own land, helped launch the Indian Independence Movement. By the turn of the 20th century, with industrialization and the discovery of additional salt deposits, global salt production exploded and the price dropped. Soon, salt producers were harvesting millions of metric tons of salt every year, using the same methods that have been honed for thousands of years, only with the help of more sophisticated and explosive tools. And sprinkling this abundance of salt on every meal was easier than ever before thanks to the Morton Salt Company, who in 1911, began adding magnesium carbonate to prevent salt from clumping in humid kitchens. With salt free to flow, the invention of salt and pepper shakers followed. The pair became seasoning soulmates, regularly adorning tabletops across North America and Europe.
