Order, order! Who do we have on the stand today? Queen Victoria... wasn’t she the definition of straight laced? Indeed, Your Honor. Throughout her 63-year reign, she was a tireless model of morality and restraint. So much so that the 19th century is popularly called the “Victorian Age” and is still associated with those very qualities. Unfortunately, this era is also associated with rigid gender roles, deep class divisions, and the subjugation of colonized peoples— all reinforced by Queen Victoria's so-called virtues. Her moralistic approach to monarchy was more concerned with making the British Empire look like a big happy family than actually helping anyone inside it. I guess appearances can be deceiving. In her defense, Your Honor, Victoria came to the throne at the tender age of 18 and faced immediate pressure to restore public faith in the crown. The previous two kings had been drinkers, womanizers, and gamblers. Meanwhile, Victoria was expected to serve as the country's moral compass during the dizzying changes brought by industrialization. Well, she certainly got her chance. Her reign coincided with the birth of mass media, giving her the power to exert immense cultural influence. In newspapers, she appeared as the perfect wife to her beloved Prince Albert. Press images of her pure white wedding dress started a fashion trend that continues to this day. And front pages featuring their family’s idyllic Christmas celebrations set a new standard for middle class society. Specifically a standard that shamed any family who couldn’t afford a picture-perfect holiday. She had to cultivate the image of a dutiful wife and dedicated mother to meet popular expectations— even though her relationship with her children was sometimes stormy. Besides, she wasn't entirely traditional. While religious leaders and some doctors argued against using pain medication during childbirth, Victoria made the practice socially acceptable by using anesthesia during the births of two of her children. And she publicly supported the entry of women into the field of nursing. She also opposed giving women the right to vote, and believed they should focus on their household responsibilities instead of politics. She viewed her position as a powerful woman as an accident of birth, not as a model for her gender. Well, that's not exactly the most inspiring message. Victoria simply mirrored and reinforced larger cultural trends; she didn’t create them. Besides, her public persona was the only reliable tool she had for affecting change in Britain. As a constitutional monarch, the Queen was expected to act on the advice of her Prime Minister. And while some of the ministers she worked with during her reign actively encouraged her to shape Britain’s politics, others, like Lord John Russell, dismissed her ideas. Yet, when Russell’s government failed to address the Irish Potato Famine, Victoria received the blame. In fact, she’s still known as the “Famine Queen” to this day. That moniker may have more to do with her measly personal donation of £2,000 to famine relief, which was less than 1% of her annual stipend. And when the Ottoman sultan wanted to donate £10,000, the British Embassy talked him down to 1,000 so his generosity wouldn’t make Victoria look bad! Just like everything in her reign, Victoria cared more about her reputation than her people. She was actually very aware of how difficult her subjects’ lives were. As a child, her mother took her on tours around the UK to see how people lived. And as an adult, she read authors like Charles Dickens to better understand the lower classes. She backed social reforms like the establishment of state-funded public schools and the Public Health Act of 1875, which aimed to improve sanitation and living conditions. While I'm sure those policies helped her domestic subjects, Victoria frequently ignored her countless more international citizens. She believed that Britain should be an empire over which the sun never set, meaning she completely supported colonial expansion. It’s true— Victoria was a product of her time and believed in Britain’s imperial destiny. But she was also interested in learning about the various cultures within her empire, developing close relationships with Sarah Forbes Bonnetta, a formerly enslaved West African woman, and with Abdul Karim, an Indian secretary who taught her Hindustani. Victoria and Albert were also known abolitionists who supported deploying the Royal Navy to combat West Africa's illegal slave trade. Noble efforts, no doubt. But Victoria did nothing to counter the violent oppression and paternalistic racism that marked Britain's colonial expansion. These colonies’ inhabitants were frequently mistreated, and they could only advance so far in their country’s government, even if they were British educated. Let's not sugarcoat it. Under her watch, British forces brutally crushed uprisings in South Asia and Africa, looted resources, and enforced racial hierarchies that lasted long after her reign. Doesn’t exactly sound like a “big happy family” to me. Queen Victoria might have tried to hold her empire to a higher standard, but should we judge leaders by their ideals or their outcomes? These are the questions we have to ask when putting history on trial.
