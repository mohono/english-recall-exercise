In March 2024, US veterinarians were facing down a mystery. On dairy farms in Texas and nearby states, cows were losing their appetite and producing thick, discolored milk. At the same time, cats on these farms were being diagnosed with an unknown neurological disease. For weeks, all their tests for common cattle diseases came back negative until lab results finally uncovered the culprit: influenza A subtype H5N1 — more commonly known as bird flu. This wasn’t the first time a virus adapted for birds infected non-avian animals. In fact, every human influenza pandemic on record has been linked to some strain of bird flu. So what is it about these viruses that makes them so frighteningly flexible? Of the four groups of flu viruses, all bird flus fall into group A. Within this group, the viruses are organized into subtypes defined by two viral proteins. Hemagglutinin binds to host cell receptors, allowing the virus to enter the cell and replicate. And neuraminidase helps those new viruses leave the cell. Currently, we’ve discovered 18 types of hemagglutinin and 11 types of neuraminidase, and different subtypes of influenza A use different combinations. For example, the combo of hemagglutinin 5 and neuraminidase 1 is called H5N1. These proteins are necessary for influenza A to enter and exit host cells, so they generally determine which animals a strain can infect. But birds, especially wild aquatic birds, have receptors for most known versions of these proteins, making them susceptible to nearly all combinations. Worse still, these waterfowl shed the virus through their feces into lakes and ponds, increasing the odds of infection for any bird using those bodies of water. And since the viruses don’t usually make these birds actively sick, they can act as reservoirs for disease— flying around and spreading their strain. Thankfully, humans are mostly safe from all these flus. We only have receptors for a few viral proteins, protecting us from most combinations. And while bird flus can sometimes bind to receptors in our airways, our more vulnerable receptors are deep in our lungs. This makes infection less likely and transmission less efficient compared to common human flus that target receptors in our nose and throat. Unfortunately, influenza has other tricks up its sleeves. Viruses have antigens— specific molecules that our immune system can recognize and attack to fight against an infection. But two processes are constantly changing these molecules and challenging our immune systems. The first is antigenic drift, where antigens change slowly over time due to random mutations. This makes it harder for immune systems to recognize the virus, but the process is relatively slow and trackable. In fact, we manage the antigenic drift of human flu by updating our flu shots every year. The much more sudden and potentially dangerous process is antigenic shift. This happens when two or more different influenza viruses meet inside a host and swap entire gene segments, instantly creating a new virus with all-new abilities— including the potential to bind to different receptors and infect different species. This is why bird flus are so concerning. Since birds are vulnerable to so many influenza A subtypes, they’re natural reservoirs for antigenic shifts. Consider our dairy farm mystery. While H5N1 used to infect mostly birds, in the past few years, it’s been found in over 70 mammal species, including cats, dairy cows, foxes, seals, and bears. Each new mammalian infection gives the virus more chances to drift or shift, potentially spilling over into humans, and igniting a pandemic. And since our immune systems aren’t trained to recognize avian influenza proteins, the effects of these viruses can be deadly. So, should we panic? Not yet. Despite the fact that influenza is ubiquitous in birds across the globe, bird flu infections in humans are still relatively rare. What's most important is to stay vigilant. Scientists are working around the clock to monitor ongoing viral evolution, test vaccines, and strengthen biosecurity. Everything could change with a single outbreak, so the only way to win this viral arms race is to stay one flap ahead.
